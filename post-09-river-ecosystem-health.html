River Ecosystem Health Indicators

By Isaac Bright Addo · November 25, 2026 · Eco-Hydrology & Ecosystems

The last article on this blog looked at managing a whole watershed: the land, the forests, the farms and towns that all drain to one river. That raises an obvious question. Once a catchment is being managed, how do you know if it is working? A river will not send a report. But it does leave signs — in its water, in the living things it can still support, and in the shape and rhythm of its flow. Reading those signs is what river health indicators are for.

Why one measurement is never enough

It is tempting to judge a river by a single clear-looking sample. The water is transparent, so the river must be fine. That test fails in both directions. Clear water can carry dissolved fertiliser, pesticides or metals that no eye can see. And a river can look muddy after rain for entirely natural reasons while remaining healthy underneath.

River health is not one thing, so no one indicator can stand for it. Scientists who assess rivers therefore read several kinds of sign together — chemical, biological and physical — and look for a pattern, the way a doctor uses more than a temperature before deciding someone is well.

The chemical signs

The oldest indicators are measurements of the water itself. Dissolved oxygen is one of the most telling: most river life needs it, and it falls when pollution feeds bacteria that consume oxygen as they break waste down. A river low in oxygen at dawn, when plants have not yet replaced what the night used, is a river under strain.

Alongside oxygen come nutrients — nitrogen and phosphorus. In small amounts they feed the algae and plants everything else eats. In excess, from fertiliser or sewage, they feed too much: algal growth surges, then dies and rots, and the oxygen crash follows. Acidity, measured as pH, and temperature complete the basic picture. Many species tolerate only a narrow range of each, so a shift in either quietly decides who can still live there.

These measurements are precise, and instruments can now record some of them continuously. Their weakness is timing. A chemical sample is a snapshot: it shows the river at the moment the bottle was filled, and it can miss a pollution discharge that passed the week before.

The biological signs: the river keeps its own record

That weakness is why biologists pay close attention to the living community itself. The animals in a river have been there through everything the river has carried for weeks or months. In that sense they are a record no snapshot can match.

The classic indicators are the small invertebrates — mayfly, stonefly and caddisfly larvae among them. These groups are sensitive: they need well-oxygenated, unpolluted water, so their presence is good news and their absence is a warning. Other creatures, such as certain worms and midge larvae, tolerate polluted, low-oxygen water well. A sample dominated by the tolerant few, with the sensitive groups missing, tells a clear story even if that day's chemistry looks acceptable.

Fish add a longer view. A river that still holds its expected native species, in a range of ages — young fish as well as old — is a river where spawning, food and shelter are all still working. A river holding only a few hardy species, or fish of a single age, is showing that one of those links has broken. Plants and algae matter too: a healthy river has them, but in balance; a blanket of one fast-growing species usually means nutrients have tipped that balance.

The physical signs: flow and shape

A river is also a physical structure, and its condition shows there too. Flow is the first sign. Every river has a natural rhythm — high water in the wet season, lower flows in the dry months — and its species are timed to that rhythm. Dams, abstractions and sealed ground can flatten or scramble it: floods arrive faster and sharper off hard surfaces, while dry-season flows fall lower than the life in the channel evolved for. A river can have clean water and still be failing on flow alone.

The second sign is the channel and its edges. Banks shaded and held by roots stay cool and stable; banks stripped bare erode, widening and shallowing the channel and filling it with silt that smothers the gravel where insects live and fish spawn. Connected floodplains and wetlands — the subjects of earlier articles in this series — are part of the same picture: a river cut off from them has lost its room to spread, slow down and clean itself.

Putting the signs together

No single one of these indicators decides the verdict, and that is the point. Chemical readings can look fine while the insects say otherwise. Insects can be recovering while the flow regime still prevents fish from breeding. Health assessments therefore combine the signs into an overall picture, often comparing a river against the condition expected for an undisturbed river of its type in that region. The comparison matters: a slow lowland river and a cold mountain stream are healthy in different ways, and neither should be judged by the other's standard.

Read over years rather than weeks, these indicators do something else as well. They show direction. Is the oxygen holding through summer? Are the sensitive insects returning after a restoration project? Is the dry-season flow still falling? A catchment plan — like the one the previous article described — succeeds or fails in exactly these trends, long before the change is obvious to anyone standing on the bank.

That, in the end, is what the indicators are for. They turn "the river seems fine" into something that can be checked, argued about honestly, and acted on while there is still time. A river that is quietly failing rarely announces it any other way.
