10 Nature-Based Solutions Fighting Climate Change

By Isaac Bright Addo · November 19, 2026 · Climate Adaptation & Resilience

In the first post on this blog, we looked at what climate adaptation means. Today we meet its most hopeful family of tools. When engineers face a flooding river, the usual answer is concrete: higher walls, bigger drains. Nature-based solutions ask a different question — what would the landscape do if we let it help? Very often, the answer protects us better, costs less to maintain, and gives wildlife a home at the same time.

What "nature-based" really means

A nature-based solution protects, restores or carefully manages an ecosystem so that it solves a human problem: flooding, heat, drought, erosion, dirty water. The ecosystem is not decoration around the real work. The ecosystem is the work. Here are ten of the most proven.

1. Mangrove conservation

Mangroves are trees that live in salty coastal water, standing on tangled roots. Those roots slow down storm waves and hold the shoreline together, while the forest shelters young fish that local families depend on. Where mangroves have been cut for shrimp farms or fuel, coasts flood more easily. Where communities replant and protect them, the shore defends itself.

2. Wetland restoration

A wetland is a sponge the size of a landscape. It takes in floodwater, holds it, and lets it go slowly — and while the water sits there, plants and soils filter it cleaner. Many wetlands were drained for farming or building, and their towns now flood faster and dirtier. Restoring even part of a wetland gives the water back its waiting room.

3. Forest restoration and reforestation

Forests cool the land, call down and hold rainfall, anchor soil on slopes, and store carbon in wood and earth. Restoring a degraded forest — especially with a mix of native trees rather than one planted species in rows — protects the streams below it and the farms around it. The key word is restoration: helping a real forest come back, not just counting seedlings planted.

4. Peatland protection and rewetting

Peatlands are waterlogged lands where dead plants, unable to rot fully, have piled up over thousands of years — locking away enormous stores of carbon. When peatlands are drained, that carbon escapes and the dry peat can even catch fire. Keeping peat wet, or rewetting what was drained, is one of the quietest and most powerful climate actions on Earth.

5. River and floodplain restoration

For a century we straightened rivers and walled them in, and the water simply arrived downstream faster and higher. Restoring bends, reconnecting rivers to their floodplains, and giving floodwater safe places to spread out — the idea behind the Netherlands' "Room for the River" programme — slows the flood and recharges groundwater at the same time.

6. Urban green infrastructure

Cities overheat because concrete and asphalt store the sun. Street trees, parks, green roofs and planted rain gardens shade the streets, cool the air, and soak up storm rain where it falls instead of overwhelming the drains. On the hottest days, a tree-lined street is not prettier decoration — it is measurably cooler, and that saves lives.

7. Living shorelines and dune protection

Instead of a concrete sea wall, a living shoreline uses marsh grasses, oyster reefs, sand dunes and beach plants to absorb wave energy. Unlike a wall, which waves eventually undermine, a healthy dune or marsh can grow and adjust as seas rise — a defence that repairs itself.

8. Agroforestry and climate-smart farming

On farms, trees and crops can work together: trees shade coffee and cocoa, shelter livestock, hold soil and moisture, and give farmers fruit, fodder or timber when a crop fails. Add cover crops, mulching and careful water harvesting, and the farm becomes more resilient to both drought and downpour — while storing carbon in the bargain.

9. Grassland and rangeland restoration

Healthy grasslands hold their soil with deep roots and store carbon underground where fires cannot easily release it. Restoring overgrazed or eroded grasslands — with better grazing rotations and native grasses — protects herders' livelihoods, keeps dust down, and helps rain soak in rather than run off.

10. Watershed and source-water protection

Every town drinks from a watershed — the land that gathers its water. Protecting the forests and wetlands of that upstream land, and working with the farmers who live there, keeps reservoirs and wells cleaner and fuller. Cities around the world have learned it is cheaper to protect the watershed than to build ever-bigger treatment plants.

The honest challenges

Nature-based solutions are powerful, but they are not magic, and honesty matters. They take time: a planted mangrove forest needs years before it guards a coast. They take land, and land is contested — a wetland restored for flood protection may be land someone hoped to farm, so local people must be partners and beneficiaries, not bystanders. They need care: a restored ecosystem that nobody maintains quietly fails. And they have limits: beyond a certain level of warming and sea-level rise, ecosystems themselves come under stress, which is why they complement — never replace — cutting emissions and, where needed, sound engineering.

Done well, though, these solutions share one quality no concrete wall can match: they get better with age. A forest deepens, a wetland thickens, a dune grows. In a century that will ask a great deal of our landscapes, working with nature is not the soft option. It is the durable one.
