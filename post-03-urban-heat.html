How Cities Are Adapting to Extreme Heat

By Isaac Bright Addo · November 19, 2026 · Climate Adaptation & Resilience

Stand in a city centre on a hot afternoon, then drive twenty minutes into the countryside. The air itself feels different — because it is. Cities run hotter than the land around them, sometimes by several degrees, and on heatwave nights the gap matters most, because the city never fully cools down. As heatwaves grow longer and stronger, learning to cool our cities has become one of the most urgent tasks in climate adaptation.

Understanding the urban heat island

The effect has a name: the urban heat island. It happens for simple reasons. Concrete and asphalt drink in the sun all day and release the heat slowly all night. Buildings block the wind that would carry heat away. Cars, factories and air conditioners pour out waste heat of their own — the air conditioner cooling one room warms the street outside it. And the surfaces that once cooled the land — soil, grass, trees, whose shade and evaporating water act like natural air conditioning — have been paved over.

Heat is not shared equally. The hottest districts are usually the poorest: fewer trees, more asphalt, smaller and more crowded homes. The people most at risk are the elderly, young children, outdoor workers, and anyone whose body or medicine makes heat harder to bear. Cooling a city is therefore not only an engineering task. It is a fairness task.

Green roofs: gardens in the sky

A roof is the first surface the sun strikes, and a bare roof can become fiercely hot. A green roof covers it with soil and plants instead. The plants shade the roof, their water cools the air as it evaporates, and the soil insulates the rooms below — so the building needs less air conditioning, which means less waste heat blown into the street. Green roofs also hold rainwater back during storms, a bonus we met in the last post's look at urban green infrastructure. Their challenges are honest ones: they cost more to build, they add weight an old building may not bear, and like every garden, they need care.

Cool pavements and cool roofs

Dark asphalt absorbs sunlight; pale surfaces reflect it. Cool pavements use lighter materials or reflective coatings so roads and pavements store less heat, and the same idea — a white or reflective cool roof — is one of the cheapest cooling measures a building owner can take. Permeable pavements add a second trick: they let water soak in and evaporate later, cooling as it goes. Cool surfaces are no complete answer on their own — reflected heat has to go somewhere, and glare matters — but as part of a package they take real degrees out of a street.

Urban forests: the city's own air conditioning

Of all cooling tools, the tree is the most complete. A tree shades the ground and the wall behind it, cools the air through the water its leaves release, cleans the air, slows storm water, and gives birds a home — and people consistently report that green streets simply feel better to live on. This is why so many cities now treat their urban forest as infrastructure, like water pipes: they map where canopy is thin, plant where the need is greatest, and — the part most often forgotten — budget to water and care for young trees until they can stand on their own. A planted tree that dies in its second summer cools nothing.

Three cities, three lessons

Singapore decided that a dense city could also be a green one. Greenery is woven into the buildings themselves — sky gardens, planted walls, parks connected by green corridors — and new developments must replace the greenery their footprint removed. The lesson: in a land-scarce city, nature does not need spare land; it needs a place in the design rules.

Vienna shows the power of a plan aimed at people. The Austrian capital has a dedicated strategy against urban heat: more green space and trees, drinking fountains and misting points in public squares, and "cool streets" where traffic gives way to shade, water and places to sit during the hottest weeks. The lesson: cooling works best when it is planned city-wide and aimed first at the districts and people who suffer most.

Los Angeles is testing surfaces at scale. The city has coated stretches of road with a light, reflective "cool pavement" treatment, set targets for shade and tree canopy in its hottest neighbourhoods, and written cool roofs into its building rules. The lesson: measure as you go. Los Angeles monitors its cooled streets, so the city learns which tools earn their cost — adaptation guided by evidence, not fashion.

Cooling is a system, not a gadget

No single measure defeats a heatwave. The cities making progress combine them all — trees and green roofs for shade and evaporation, pale surfaces where planting cannot go, water features and open space for relief, and early-warning systems and cool public refuges for the days when even a greener city runs hot. The encouraging truth is that almost every cooling measure pays twice: the same tree that cools a street also cleans its air, calms its traffic and lifts the spirits of the people who live there.
