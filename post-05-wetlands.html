The Role of Wetlands in Climate Resilience

By Isaac Bright Addo · November 20, 2026 · Eco-Hydrology & Ecosystems

The previous article looked at how drought-prone regions conserve water. This one turns to a place that conserves water without being asked: the wetland. A marsh, a floodplain, a peat bog or a mangrove fringe does quietly, every day, much of what engineers are paid to build — it holds floodwater back, cleans water as it passes, and keeps rivers flowing longer into the dry season. In a changing climate, that quiet work is worth a great deal.

What a wetland is

A wetland is land that is wet enough, for long enough, to shape the life on it — soil saturated or flooded for part or all of the year, and plants and animals adapted to those conditions. Wetlands take many forms: freshwater marshes and swamps, floodplains beside rivers, peatlands in cool northern regions, and coastal salt marshes and mangroves where fresh water meets the sea. They are not wasteland between water and land. They are the working edge where the two meet, and that edge is exactly why they do so much.

A sponge against floods

When heavy rain falls on hard, drained or paved land, the water runs off fast and arrives downstream all at once — as a flood. A wetland upstream changes that timing. Its plants slow the water, its soil and shallow basins hold it, and it is released gradually over days rather than hours. Floodplains do the same work at river scale: given room to spread, a river in flood drops its peak and its speed. Communities that drained their wetlands for building or farming often discover, expensively, that they also drained away this free flood protection — and then have to replace it with walls and pumps.

Nature's water treatment

Water moves slowly through a wetland, and slow water cleans itself. Sediment settles out instead of clouding the river. Plant roots and the microbes living around them take up excess nutrients — nitrogen and phosphorus from farm runoff and wastewater — that would otherwise feed algal blooms downstream. That is why the topics roadmap for this blog lists wetlands as nature's water treatment systems, and why some cities now build artificial wetlands on purpose to polish treated wastewater before it returns to a river. The East Kolkata Wetlands in India are a well-known working example: a large wetland system that treats much of the city's wastewater while supporting fish ponds and vegetable growing on the nutrients it captures.

A buffer in drought, and a store of carbon

The water a wetland holds in the wet season does not vanish in the dry one. It leaks slowly into groundwater and feeds springs and streams long after rain has stopped — a natural savings account of exactly the kind the previous article described people building with tanks and check dams. Wetlands also store carbon on a remarkable scale. In waterlogged soil, dead plant material decays very slowly, so carbon stays locked away instead of returning to the air. Peatlands are the extreme case: they cover a small share of the land surface but hold a very large share of the world's soil carbon. Drain a peatland and that store is released; protect or rewet it and the store stays put.

A home that supports livelihoods

Wetlands are among the most productive ecosystems on earth. They are nurseries for fish, feeding and breeding grounds for birds, and a source of reeds, grazing, wild foods and clean water for nearby communities. Protecting a wetland is therefore not only a climate measure. For many communities it protects food and income directly — one reason conservation that ignores local livelihoods so often fails, and conservation built with the community tends to last.

Why wetlands are being lost — and what restoration looks like

For much of the last century, wetlands were treated as land waiting to be made useful: drained for fields, filled for building, cut off from their rivers by levees. The world has lost a large share of its wetlands that way, and with them the flood control, water cleaning and carbon storage they provided free of charge. Restoration reverses the logic. It can mean rewetting a drained peatland by blocking its drainage ditches, reconnecting a river to its floodplain, restoring mangroves on a coastline, or building a new wetland where a town needs flood room and cleaner water. Restored wetlands rarely return overnight to what was lost, but their basic services — holding water, slowing it, cleaning it — begin quickly.

The landscape's own water keepers

A wetland asks for very little: room, water, and to be left connected to the river or coast it belongs to. In return it softens floods, cleans water, buffers drought, stores carbon and feeds both wildlife and people. That is why I place wetlands near the centre of climate resilience on this blog.
